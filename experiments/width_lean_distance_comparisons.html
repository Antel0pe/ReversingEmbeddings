1 · Width on the x-axis, one line per lean

Left: how far each leaned image is from its same-width upright image. Right: whether the width-change vector measured at upright still works after leaning. Each curve compares corresponding settings along the sweeps.

The curves do not simply fan out at a constant rate. A larger lean often makes the response more different, but the vector mismatch can flatten or decrease over some intervals. Neither equal image distances nor similar vector lengths imply equal vectors.

2 · Use exactly the same +1° increment

A line labeled 10° → 11° uses that same angular step at every width. The first panel measures its length. The next two compare the actual vectors across widths and across starting leans. The last compares the distances to a width neighbor and a lean neighbor.

A standard vector does work locally: the 0° → 1° step matches at sampled widths 2.35–3.65 px (vector gap below 0.000001 in float32; below 0.000000000001 before float32 storage). But compare widths 3.2 and 4.6: the two step lengths are both 0.615, while the vectors are 90° apart. The same-sized edge movement is affecting different pixels.

Those inequalities apply to the stated steps and sampled points. They do not mean every pair of images differing in width is closer than every pair differing in lean.

Why equal degrees do not force equal pixel-space vectors

At a fixed row, increasing lean shifts both horizontal stroke edges by the same geometric amount, independently of width. But width changes which pixels contain those edges. Equal edge shifts can therefore produce different pixel-change vectors.

horizontal shift at vertical coordinate y = (14 − y) × [tan(new lean) − tan(old lean)]

The tangent above is the trigonometric function; all angles here are converted from degrees. Equal increments of lean angle also produce larger geometric shifts at larger positive angles.

The actual knob operations commute: widening then leaning and leaning then widening reach the same D. The two original root vectors do not generally add up to D. They predict B + C − A; the gap from that prediction to D is exactly the mixed difference above.

Adding two fixed pixel vectors also commutes. The nonzero gap shows that those fixed vectors do not describe the state-dependent changes needed to follow the family.

The three images A, B, and C alone do not determine D. Here we obtain D from the renderer. Predicting it from those three observations would require an additional rule for the interaction.

What was checked

57 widths × 91 leans sample the whole allowed width/lean rectangle. A refined grid halves both spacings and reproduces every matching image exactly. The two mixed-difference formulas agree; both actual parameter orders reach bit-identical rendered endpoints. The finer grid checks the sampled proximity ordering. All calculations use the Python renderer, with its 16-subrow coverage model.

The plots show corresponding-setting distances, not the closest distance between whole curves. They describe this fixed-center, fixed-height slice. Float32 images are subtracted and measured in float64.
